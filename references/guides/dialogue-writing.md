# Dialogue Writing Guide

Good dialogue is a powerful tool for revealing character, advancing plot, and creating conflict.

---

## Core Principles of Dialogue

### 1. Dialogue Must Have Purpose

Every line should accomplish at least one of the following:

| Purpose | Example |
|-----|------|
| **Advance the plot** | "I found the murder weapon, in the grass by the river." |
| **Reveal character** | "I don't trust the police. They never helped anyone like me." |
| **Create conflict** | "You lied to me. You were lying to me the whole time." |
| **Convey information** | "The bomb will detonate at three o'clock." |
| **Express emotion** | "I... I don't know what to say." |
| **Build suspense** | "Do you know what really happened that night?" |

**Ineffective dialogue:**
> "Hi."
> "Hi."
> "How are you?"
> "Fine."

### 2. Dialogue Should Be Concise

People don't speak in essays.

| Wordy | Concise |
|-----|------|
| "What I want to tell you is that I think we should leave this place immediately." | "We've got to go. Now." |
| "I'm very sorry, but I really don't know the answer to the thing you just asked." | "I don't know." |

### 3. Real People Don't Speak in Complete Sentences

Real conversation is full of interruptions, hesitations, topic shifts, unfinished thoughts, and implications rather than direct statements.

**Example:**
> "I was going to tell you, but—"
> "But what?"
> "Forget it. It's nothing."
> "No. Tell me."
> "It's really nothing."

## Dialogue Formatting

### Punctuation and Formatting

```
"Keep the dialogue," he said.
"Is this a question?" she asked.
"Hit it!" he shouted.
```

### Using Dialogue Tags

- Omit the tag when the speaker is clear
- Use neutral tags like "said" and "asked"
- Avoid overloading tags with adverbs

| Overdone | Improved |
|---------|--------|
| "You lied to me," he said angrily. | "You lied to me." His voice was shaking. |
| "Okay," she agreed happily. | Her eyes lit up. "Okay." |

### Paragraphing

Start a new paragraph each time the speaker changes.

## Subtext

In good dialogue, the real meaning lives beneath the surface.

### Direct vs. Subtext

| Direct (dull) | Subtext (interesting) |
|-------------|---------------|
| "I'm angry." | "I'm fine. Really, I'm fine. Truly." |
| "I like you." | "You look... nice today." |
| "I don't trust you." | "Thanks for telling me. I'll remember that." |

### Subtext Techniques

**1. Changing the subject**
```
"Do you love me?"

"Did you see the forecast? It's supposed to rain tomorrow."
```

**2. Answering with a question**
```
"Did you kill him?"

"Do you think someone like me would do something like that?"
```

**3. Talking about something else**
```
"Do you miss me?"

"My mom called yesterday."
```

**4. Silence and action**
```
"Can you forgive me?"

She went on reading her magazine and turned the page.
```

---

## Combining Dialogue with Action

Action that matches or contradicts the words both create dramatic effect.

**Matches (reinforces):**
> "I love you." She hugged him tightly, tears spilling down her face.

**Contradicts (reveals the truth):**
> "I completely support you." His gaze slid away, his fist clenching in his pocket.

**Action replacing tags:**
```
"You're lying." She slammed her hand on the table.

"Sit down." He didn't look up.
```

## Advanced Dialogue Rhythm

Dialogue isn't just content — the rhythm itself is expression.

### Fast-Paced Dialogue

**Traits:** short lines, no tags, interruptions, rapid escalation
**Best for:** arguments, chases, urgent decisions

```
"Left!"
"Too late—"
"Jump!"
The water rushed up to meet them.
"Hold on!"
```

**How to achieve it:**
- Strip out all dialogue tags (the reader can tell who's speaking)
- One line per paragraph, under fifteen words
- Allow incomplete sentences
- Use action in place of breathing room

### Slow-Paced Dialogue

**Traits:** long sentences, pauses, circumlocution, rich subtext
**Best for:** confessions, probing, interrogation, farewells

```
"All these years," he said, picking up his teacup and watching the leaves slowly sink, "did you ever think that if we hadn't taken that road back then—"

"No." She answered quickly. Too quickly.

He set the cup down. "Me neither."
Silence spread out. Outside the window, the plane-tree leaves were lifted by the wind, then fell back down.
"Actually," she said, her voice dropping, "sometimes."

```

**How to achieve it:**
- Insert action description and silence between the lines
- Let characters circle around a subject, almost speaking then pulling back
- Use environmental detail to control the breathing rhythm
- Hold back key information

### The Dramatic Power of Silence

The most powerful thing in dialogue is the part **not said.**

```
"Did you know all along?"
He didn't answer. He picked up his glass of water, drank, set it down, picked it up again.

"I'm asking you a question."
At last he looked at her. "Do you really want to hear the answer?"
```

Uses of silence:
- **Refusing to answer** → implies concealment or protection
- **Delayed reaction** → implies shock or calculation
- **Interrupted speech** → implies emotional collapse or resolve
- **Answering with action** → more powerful than words

### The Power Play in Dialogue

Dialogue is an arena of power. Who asks, who dodges, who interrupts, who stays silent — every choice signals standing.

| Power signal | High-status character | Low-status character |
|---------|---------|---------|
| Ask vs. answer | Asks (controls the topic) | Forced to answer |
| Interrupt vs. be interrupted | Interrupts freely | Careful not to interrupt |
| Topic control | Decides what's discussed | Passively follows |
| Use of silence | Uses silence to pressure | Uses silence to escape |

**Example (interrogation):**
```
"Where were you that night?"

"Home."

"Can anyone confirm that?"

"...No."

"You were alone?"

"Yes."

"The whole night?"
```

**Example (power reversed):**
```
"You should tell me the truth."

"Should?" She laughed. "What right do you have to use the word 'should' with me?"
He found himself wordless.

"Get your own affairs in order before you come asking about mine."
```

## Dialogue Scene Types

### Argument Scene

**Traits:** short lines, interruptions, repeated emphasis, escalating emotion

```
"You promised!"

"Things changed!"

"That's just an excuse!"

"You don't understand!"

"Of course I don't! You never tell me anything!"
```

### Confession Scene

**Traits:** hesitation, pauses, searching for words, sincerity or awkwardness

```
"I... I want to say... all these years, I've been thinking—if we..."

She lowered her head, her voice growing small.

"If we what?"

"If we'd met each other sooner."
```

### Ambiguous/Flirting Scene

**Traits:** double meanings, testing, implication, reading between the lines

```
"You look nice today."

"Only today?"

"Hmm... especially nice today."

"Should I be worried about tomorrow, then?"

"Tomorrow... let's see about tomorrow."
```

### Farewell Scene

**Traits:** the unsaid, restraint, currents running under a calm surface

```
"I guess this is it, then."

"Yeah."

"Take care of yourself."

"You too."
She turned and walked two steps, then stopped. "Remember to water the jasmine. Don't forget again."

"I won't."
She didn't turn back.
```

## Common Dialogue Problems

### Avoid

1. **Info dumping** — characters stating what they both already know
   > Wrong: "As you know, our company was founded in 1995..."

2. **Everyone sounds the same** — characters are indistinguishable
   > Fix: give each character a distinctive way of speaking (word choice, sentence rhythm, tone, verbal tics)

3. **Over-politeness** — real speech is rougher
   > Wrong: "I'm terribly sorry to bother you — might I ask whether you would be..."
   > Right: "Hey. Do me a favor."

4. **Meaningless small talk** — unless it serves a purpose

5. **Preaching** — characters delivering long philosophical lectures

## Dialogue Self-Review

Check after writing each exchange:

- [ ] Does every line have a purpose?
- [ ] Would the plot be affected if I deleted it?
- [ ] Can I identify the speaker without looking at the tags?
- [ ] Is there subtext?
- [ ] Is the rhythm right (fast/slow)?
- [ ] Are the tags used with restraint?
