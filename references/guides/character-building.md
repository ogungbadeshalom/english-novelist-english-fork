# Character-Building Guide

Good characters are the soul of a story. Readers remember people, not plots.

> For the character-profile template, use [character-template.md](character-template.md) (create it during Phase 2 planning). This guide focuses on techniques for **building characters in the writing itself**.

---

## Core Principle: Contradiction Creates Depth

Real people are complex and full of contradictions. Contradiction is the **engine of character growth**.

**Effective contradictions:**
- The hard-driving female police chief who, at home, talks to her dead husband's photograph
- The scholar who spouts virtue by day, and secretly writes divorce papers for money at night
- The cold-blooded killer who brings fish for the stray cats every day
- The man who claims he doesn't believe in fate — and keeps a charmed talisman in his drawer

**Ineffective contradiction:** contradiction for its own sake, with no connection to the character's core.

## Revealing Character Indirectly

Don't state a personality flat out; show it through behavior. This is the essence of **Show, Don't Tell** applied to character.

### Replace Labels with Action

| Direct statement (weak) | Indirect showing (strong) |
|-------------------------|---------------------------|
| He was furious | He crushed the paper cup in his hand; shards bit into his palm and he didn't let go |
| She was nervous | She adjusted her glasses — a third time now |
| He was arrogant | He never met his subordinates' eyes; he always looked out the window midsentence |
| She was kind | She fed the strays for three years and never told a soul |
| He was lonely | The TV was always on. He never watched it |

### Reveal Character Through Choices

What a character picks under pressure says more than any description.

**Example:**
> In the burning house he could save the painting, or the cat. He carried out the cat.
> No need to write "he was kind" — the choice already said it.

### Expose Truth Through Habits

Recurring small habits are the character's unconscious self-revelation.

- Touching the ring finger when nervous (did it once hold a ring?)
- Always sitting with his back to a wall (insecure?)
- Avoiding eye contact when speaking (hiding something?)
- Thanking every waiter (upbringing — or guilt?)

## Building the Protagonist

### The Protagonist Must Have

- **A clear goal** — what they want
- **A strong motive** — why they want it
- **Relatability** — the reader can understand their feelings
- **Room to grow** — they change over the course of the story

### Protagonist Archetypes

| Type | Traits | Function in the story |
|------|--------|-----------------------|
| Hero | Brave, just, selfless | Defeats an external threat |
| Growing | Weak to strong | Overcomes an internal flaw |
| Anti-hero | Morally grey, complex | Challenges conventional morality |
| Everyman | An ordinary person swept into extraordinary events | Strong reader identification |

### Making the Flaw Fatal

The protagonist must have a flaw, and that flaw must cause a real failure at a critical moment. A character with no flaw never grows.

| Flaw | The failure it causes | Direction of growth |
|------|-----------------------|---------------------|
| Arrogance | Underestimates the opponent, walks into a trap | Learning to listen |
| Distrust | Refuses help, is left isolated | Learning to trust |
| Perfectionism | Can't act in time, misses the chance | Learning to accept imperfection |
| Vengefulness | Is used, loses control | Learning to let go |
| Avoidance | Lets problems snowball | Learning to face things |

## Building the Antagonist

A good villain isn't "evil" — they're a person with a **different set of beliefs**.

### Standards for a Good Antagonist

- **Strong and credible** — not a joke or a straw man
- **Has their own logic** — they believe they're doing the right thing
- **Deeply connected to the protagonist** — not evil for its own sake
- **Fleshes out the theme** — challenges the protagonist's beliefs

### Layers of Villain Motive

**Shallow motive** (not enough):
> "I want to destroy the world" → flat, boring

**Mid-level motive** (adequate):
> "I lost everything; the world means nothing to me" → understandable but thin

**Deep motive** (powerful):
> "My daughter died of an illness that was treatable — but the pharmaceutical company delayed approval to protect profits. This world isn't worth saving." → The reader doesn't agree, but understands.

### The Mirror Relationship Between Protagonist and Antagonist

The best villain is the protagonist's **dark mirror** — if the protagonist had gone down a different road, they'd have become this.

```
Protagonist: after losing his family, chose to protect others
Antagonist:  after losing his family, chose to destroy everything
Same starting point, different choices.
```

## The Function of Supporting Characters

The only reason a supporting character exists is to **have a function in the protagonist's story**.

### Types of Supporting Characters

| Type | Function | Caution |
|------|----------|---------|
| Mentor | Guides the protagonist, passes on information | Must have their own blind spots |
| Ally | Assists and supports the protagonist | Must not be a mere tool/plot device |
| Comic relief | Relieves tension | Must be capable of seriousness at the key moment |
| Traitor | Creates a turn of events | The betrayal needs prior setup |

### Principles for Supporting Characters

- Cut any character who "just exists"
- Every supporting character must have a clear function
- Avoid stereotypes (unless it's a deliberate choice)

## Designing Character Relationships

### Relationships Change Over Time

A good relationship develops with the story rather than staying static:

```
Ch. 1: Strangers
Ch. 3: Reluctant allies
Ch. 5: Trust is building
Ch. 7: Betrayal / the test
Final ch.: True friendship (or estrangement)
```

### Revealing Relationship Depth in Layers

Reveal a relationship's depth gradually — don't expose everything at once:
- **Surface**: everyday interaction
- **Middle**: tacit understanding built through shared experience
- **Deep**: real feelings / secrets

### High-Drama Relationships

| Relationship | Dramatic potential | Classic pattern |
|--------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Frenemies | High | "I know you're using me, but I need you" |
| Forbidden love | High | Opposing identities/stances, but feelings they can't control |
| Mentor–student | Medium | The latent tension of the student eventually surpassing the master |
| Sibling rivalry | Medium | Same start, different choices, rising and falling against each other |

## Introducing a Character

### Principles for a First Appearance

An effective introduction:
- **In action** — shows ability or personality
- **In conflict** — establishes stakes or opposition immediately
- **In a misunderstanding** — creates suspense

### First-Appearance Comparisons

| Ineffective | Effective |
|-------------|-----------|
| Li Ming, 28, is a detective. He walks into his office. | Li Ming ducked under the police tape; an officer moved to block him. "Detective Li, city Homicide." He flashed his badge and walked straight to the body. |
| A beautiful girl sat by the window. Her name was Sonia. | She hadn't slept in three days; the coffee in her cup trembled. When the doorbell rang, she nearly dropped the cup. |

**Avoid:** describing a face in a mirror, resume-style introductions, and meaningless daily-arounds.

## Consistency Checks

A character's behavior must match the personality you've established.

**Check questions:**
- Does this fit their core values?
- Given their background, would they react this way?
- How would their fears influence this decision?
- What mistake would their flaw lead them into?

**Handling exceptions:**
- If a character acts "out of character," there must be a reason
- The explanation should arrive in the same or the next chapter
- It can be a sign of growth (the character overcoming a flaw)
