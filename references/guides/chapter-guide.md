# Chapter Writing Guide

Every chapter should be a complete narrative unit while pushing the overall story forward.

---

## The First 20% Decides Everything

**Readers decide within the first 20% whether to keep reading.** If the opening doesn't grab them, they walk away — no matter how good the rest is.

### What the First 20% Must Achieve

1. **Immediate tension** — the reader feels a crisis or conflict right away
2. **Significant event** — something important happens that moves the plot
3. **Emotional impact** — the reader feels something strong (curiosity, shock, dread)
4. **The urge to continue** — an urgent need to know what happens next

### Fatal Opening Mistakes (Avoid at All Costs)

| Mistake | Example | Why It's Fatal |
|---------|---------|----------------|
| Weather description | "The day was clear and cloudless..." | Unrelated to the story, the reader has no patience |
| Daily routine | "Li Ming woke up, brushed his teeth, washed his face, ate breakfast..." | Boring, no conflict |
| Recap of last chapter | "Previously, in our story..." | The reader already knows — a waste of time |
| Slow setup | "First, let me explain the background of this city..." | Info dump, no action |
| Flat dialogue | "Hi, how are you? I'm fine." | Pointless talk with no tension |
| Over-explanation | "This happened because, and so, and then..." | Telling instead of showing |

## Ten Powerful Opening Techniques

### 1. Open in the Middle of the Action (In Medias Res)

Start directly at the high point of a conflict or action.

> **Thriller/Mystery:** The bullet grazed his ear and shattered the vase behind him. Li Ming didn't look back — he rolled behind the sofa. 
>
> **Romance/Contemporary:** The zipper on the wedding dress caught. The wedding march had already begun outside the door, and on her phone screen, a text from a woman she didn't recognize was still glowing, unread.
>
> **Fantasy/Xianxia:** The moment the mountain-protection formation shattered, Su Yan knew his own sect brothers had sold him out. Behind him was a bottomless chasm; in front of him, three fourth-tier demon beasts.
>
> **Historical/Costume:** The blade flashed, and the imperial envoy's head rolled across the bluestone. Zhao Yin wiped the blood from his face and looked up at the full court of ministers kneeling before him.
>
> **Science Fiction:** The alarm screamed. Lin Wei woke from her cryo-pod to find that all six hundred and thirty-two other pods on the ship were showing "NO VITAL SIGNS."

**Why it works:** It builds tension immediately — the reader wants to know "what happened" and "what happens now."

### 2. An Abnormal Situation

Present a scene that defies logic and sparks curiosity.

> **Thriller/Mystery:** A dead man sat at his desk, writing a report.
>
> **Romance/Contemporary:** He dialed the face in the mirror. The voice on the other end said, "You have the wrong number — I don't know you." But it was his own voice.
>
> **Fantasy/Xianxia:** Every flower in the city withered in the same second. Old men in the street knelt and wept; children clapped and laughed. Only Yun Mo knew what it meant — the seal had broken.
>
> **Historical/Costume:** On the day the sun rose from the west, the royal historian burned every history book in existence.
>
> **Science Fiction:** The lie detector said she was telling the truth. But what she described couldn't happen in this universe.

**Why it works:** The impossible forces the reader to ask "what's going on?"

### 3. A Shocking Line of Dialogue

Open with a single stunning line of dialogue.

> **Thriller/Mystery:** "I sold the child." His wife said it calmly, still turning the pages of her magazine.
>
> **Romance/Contemporary:** "You never loved me, did you?" She was smiling as she said it, and her smile chilled him to the bone.
>
> **Fantasy/Xianxia:** "The tribulation failed." The elder's voice echoed. "She is no longer human."
>
> **Historical/Costume:** "I beg Your Majesty to bestow death." No one in the hall dared make a sound. The minister set his cap on the floor and knocked his forehead three times against the ground before the throne.
>
> **Science Fiction:** "We are not the first humans." For the first time, the AI's voice trembled. "We are the seventh batch. The first six destroyed themselves."

**Why it works:** It lands an emotional shock instantly, and the reader can't wait to learn the cause and consequence.

### 4. Countdown Opening

Begin from a moment of time pressure.

> **Thriller/Mystery:** Three minutes until the bomb goes off. And the defusal kit is missing one red wire.
>
> **Romance/Contemporary:** Ten minutes until the flight gate closes. She's gripping two tickets in her hand — one hers, one his.
>
> **Fantasy/Xianxia:** One incense's time before the seventh strike of heavenly thunder. His golden core hadn't condensed yet, and there was no retreat behind him.
>
> **Historical/Costume:** The secret letter must be delivered before dawn. At first light the gates open, and everyone inside will die.
>
> **Science Fiction:** Remaining oxygen: four hours, seventeen minutes. Five people on the ship. The escape pod holds three.

**Why it works:** Urgency naturally creates the drive to keep reading.

### 5. A Major Discovery

Open with the discovery of a crucial clue or truth.

> **Thriller/Mystery:** The autopsy report lay spread across the desk. Cause of death: carefully planned murder. And there was only one suspect — himself.
>
> **Romance/Contemporary:** She found a photograph in her husband's coat pocket. She didn't recognize the woman in it, but the background was her own bedroom.
>
> **Fantasy/Xianxia:** Deep in the secret realm stood a mirror. This reflection in it was of herself, a thousand years ago.
>
> **Historical/Costume:** He opened the forbidden archive and found that the battle of thirty years ago was completely different from what the histories said. The first name on the casualty list was his father's.
>
> **Science Fiction:** The data decoded at last. The signal from deep space was a set of coordinates — pointing at Earth.

**Why it works:** A major twist forces the reader to re-evaluate everything before it.

### 6. Moment of Crisis

Open at the moment a character faces their greatest crisis.

> **Thriller/Mystery:** The instant the door was kicked open, she knew the secret was finished. The contents of the safe were about to be exposed.
>
> **Romance/Contemporary:** The moment she walked toward the other man, he realized he'd never spoken the words. And now it was too late.
>
> **Fantasy/Xianxia:** The sound of his nascent soul shattering was louder than any blade sinking into flesh. Kneeling in the ruins, he felt his cultivation draining away inch by inch.
>
> **Historical/Costume:** On the seventh day of the siege, the grain ran out. The commander shared the last of the wine among the wounded and raised an empty bowl to his own lips.
>
> **Science Fiction:** The moment the engines cut, the hull began to fall toward the black hole. Everyone was screaming — except her, watching the distorted starlight out the viewport.

**Why it works:** High stakes — the reader cares what happens to the character.

### 7. A Mystery Emerges

Open with a phenomenon that defies explanation.

> **Thriller/Mystery:** When he woke up, he found a tattoo on the back of his right hand that he'd never seen before. And he had no memory of the night before.
>
> **Romance/Contemporary:** Every night at 3:07, her phone received a blank text. The sender's number was her own.
>
> **Fantasy/Xianxia:** On the third floor of the sect's library, all the writing in every ancient text vanished overnight. Only one book gained a few pages — with a single name written on them.
>
> **Historical/Costume:** The dry well had been empty for a century. This morning the water had risen, and floating on the surface was a lotus blossom that had no business blooming in winter.
>
> **Science Fiction:** The unmanned probe sent back photos of the Martian surface. In the lower-left corner of the ninth, Chinese characters had been laid out in stones.

**Why it works:** The enigma makes the reader want to solve the puzzle.

### 8. Betrayal Opening

Begin with a betrayal or the collapse of trust.

> **Thriller/Mystery:** The gun pressed into his back. "I'm sorry," said the partner he'd trusted for ten years, "they offered too much."
>
> **Romance/Contemporary:** She waited outside the civil registry office for three hours. What finally came was a text from him: "Sorry. I registered with someone else today."
>
> **Fantasy/Xianxia:** The senior disciple smiled as he drove his sword into her chest. "Did you think that's how the protection formation broke?"
>
> **Historical/Costume:** Twenty years of sworn brotherhood, and he dashed his cup to the ground and drew his blade. From this day on, only enemies — never brothers again.
>
> **Science Fiction:** The moment the loyalty protocol triggered, he understood at last — his partner had never been on the humans' side.

**Why it works:** It's an emotional shock, and the character is plunged into dire straits in an instant.

### 9. A Momentous Choice

Begin with a character's agonizing decision.

> **Thriller/Mystery:** The life raft held only one person. Her husband and her daughter were both struggling in the icy water. She reached out —
>
> **Romance/Contemporary:** The promotion notice and her surgery report landed on the desk at the same moment. She could choose only one, and there was time for only one.
>
> **Fantasy/Xianxia:** A thirty percent chance of making it through the tribulation. But if she didn't try, her golden core would shatter within half a year — a living death. She stared at the pill of unknown origin in her hand and gritted her teeth.
>
> **Historical/Costume:** Surrender, and the city's people live. Fight, and go down in history but perish with the city. He stood on the wall; he had to give his answer before sunset.
>
> **Science Fiction:** Erasing the data would save ten thousand people — but it would also wipe out every memory she had of her daughter. She stared at the delete key, her finger hovering in the air.

**Why it works:** A moral dilemma forces the reader to think "what would I choose?"

### 10. Foreshadowing the Ending

Begin at a key moment in the future, then cut back.

> **Thriller/Mystery:** Three days from now, everyone in this room will regret today's decision. But for now, everyone in the conference room is smiling.
>
> **Romance/Contemporary:** She only found the letter when she was sorting through his belongings. On the envelope: "Open after I die." The date was ten years ago.
>
> **Fantasy/Xianxia:** The histories would later record that this catastrophe began on an utterly ordinary morning. No one noticed the stranger who arrived at the city gate.
>
> **Historical/Costume:** Posterity would say that one night changed the fate of the realm. But the people who were there remember only that the moon was especially full that night, and the wine especially strong.
>
> **Science Fiction:** This is an after-action report. If you are reading it, our plan failed. It all began seventy-two hours ago.

**Why it works:** Foreshadowing disaster or a turning point, the reader wants to know how it all came to pass.

## Breaking Reader Expectations

Predictability is a novel's greatest enemy. The moment readers guess what happens next, they start skimming — then they put the book down. Here are four core techniques for breaking expectations.

### Expected-Opposite Reversal

Set up the reader to expect path A, then pivot to path B. The key: the setup for A must be convincing enough that the reader is sure they're right.

**Example:**

> [Mystery] Every piece of evidence points to the butler — motive, opportunity, fingerprints on the murder weapon. The protagonist bursts into the butler's room — and finds the butler already dead. The real killer is someone else, and the evidence was staged.
>
> [Romance] She works up the courage to confess her feelings. He's silent for a long time — and the reader assumes rejection. Then he says, "I've waited three years to hear that. But sit down — there's something I have to tell you first." His tone shifts. He's about to confess a secret.

**How to execute:**
- The setup should take at least 2–3 paragraphs so the reader is convinced they're right
- The reversal can't come from nowhere — the reader must look back and realize the clues were there all along
- The post-reversal direction must be more interesting than what the reader expected, not less

### Exploiting the Information Gap

Let the reader know something the character doesn't (generating anxiety), or let the character know something the reader doesn't (generating suspense). The asymmetry itself produces tension.

**Example:**

> [Mystery — reader knows] The reader learned in a previous chapter that the tea is poisoned. This chapter, the protagonist raises the cup and jokes with friends. Every sip raises the reader's anxiety another notch.
>
> [Fantasy — character knows] The protagonist knows the approaching caravan is an enemy scout force. But the townspeople are cheerfully setting up the market fair. He tells no one — because he's not sure who he can trust.

**How to execute:**
- The information gap shouldn't last too long (reveal or exploit it within 2–3 chapters), or the reader feels the story is stalling
- When exploiting the gap, show the unaware party's normal reaction — the contrast doubles the tension
- The moment the gap closes must produce real consequences

### Complex Motives

There's no pure good or evil. When the reader understands the antagonist's position, or realizes the protagonist's actions aren't entirely just, the story gains depth instantly.

**Example:**

> [Historical] The rebel commander lays siege and the people starve. The reader hates him. Then it's revealed: three years ago, these very townspeople's indifference let his village be slaughtered. He's come to collect the debt.
>
> [Contemporary] She's been quietly sabotaging her best friend's relationship. The reader finds her vicious. Then it's revealed: the man in that relationship was hers first — the friend knowingly stole him. She's taking back what was hers. But the methods are wrong. Both of them share the blame.

**How to execute:**
- Complex motives aren't a pass — the character still must face consequences for their actions
- Reveal the motive only after the reader has already formed a judgment; too early and it loses its impact
- The best complex motives leave the reader unsure whose side to take, even after understanding both

### An Unforeseen Complication

Introduce a new variable into what seemed like a solved problem. The reader lets out a breath — only to find the matter is far from over.

**Example:**

> [Mystery] The case is cracked, the killer confesses, the protagonist can finally rest. While cataloging evidence, he notices a detail he'd overlooked — the killer had no time window. Someone took the fall for him. The case is just beginning.
>
> [Romance] They're finally together. The misunderstanding resolved, a kiss, the perfect ending atmosphere. Her phone buzzes in her pocket. She checks it, and slowly the smile drains from her face. A pregnancy test result. The child isn't his.

**How to execute:**
- The new variable must connect logically to the plot so far — it can't come from nowhere
- Introduce it at the instant the reader has just relaxed (within 1–2 paragraphs of the resolution)
- The new variable should be bigger or more complex than the original problem — escalating layer by layer

## Classical Literary Techniques

These techniques grow out of classical literary tradition and can raise the quality of your prose to another level. These are working tools, not abstract theory.

### Plain Description (白描 / Bai Miao)

Capture the essence with the fewest possible strokes. Strip away excess adjectives and modifiers; let the picture speak for itself.

**Counter-example (ornament overload):**
> Beneath the brilliant starry sky, resplendent sunset clouds reflected on the tranquil lake while a gentle breeze brushed the emerald willows.

**Plain description:**
> The lake caught the sky's light, water unbroken. In the distance, someone lit a lamp.

**How to execute:**
- Lead with nouns and verbs; cut adjectives wherever you can
- Maximum of one modifier per sentence
- Show with action instead of adjectives: "he looked old" is weaker than "he pushed himself up off the wall"
- After writing a passage, delete every adjective and read it again — if the meaning survives, the adjectives were surplus

### Leaving Space (留白 / Liu Bai)

What you don't say carries more weight than what you do. Withhold the answer in key scenes and let the reader fill in the silence.

**How to execute:**
- Dialogue scenes: end at the moment of silence; don't write the reply
- Emotional scenes: write the physical action, not the feeling. "She folded the letter and put it back in the envelope" is stronger than "she was heartsick"
- Turning-point scenes: stop at the instant the reversal happens; pick it up in the next chapter
- Climax scenes: cut off at the highest peak — no retrospection, no summing up

**Example:**
> He watched her back disappear into the security gate. His phone buzzed in his pocket. He didn't answer it.

The reader will wonder: why didn't he answer? How did he feel? Reader participation is far stronger than stating it outright.

### Recurring Imagery

Carry an abstract emotion on the back of a concrete object that reappears. It's a shorthand symbol for feeling.

**How to execute:**
- Choose a concrete object tied to the character or theme (a dying flower, a stopped clock, rain on a windowpane)
- Let it appear naturally early on — no heavy-handed emphasis
- Vary and repeat it mid-story (the flower fresh to withered, the clock running to stopped)
- Detonate it at the critical moment, releasing all the accumulated emotional weight
- Keep imagery to three or fewer per book — much more and it becomes a codebook

**Example:** In chapter one there's a pot of pothos on her desk, being watered. In chapter ten she's hospitalized and two of its leaves have yellowed. In chapter twenty, after she's gone, all that's left on the desk is an empty pot of dried-out soil. One image told the entire emotional arc.

### Subtle Foreshadowing (草蛇灰线 / Cao She Hui Xian)

Bury clues in apparently casual details. The reader won't notice on the first pass — they'll have an aha moment on the re-read.

**How to execute:**
- Disguise the seed as an irrelevant aside (a passing mention, a background detail, an offhand gesture)
- When the payoff arrives, don't blare it — let the reader find the connection themselves
- Space the distance wide (at least five chapters)
- Keep a foreshadowing log, or you'll forget your own setups

**Example:** In chapter two, a character casually mentions "my sister." In chapter twelve, the reader learns the sister died years ago. Looking back, that offhand "my sister" betrayed that he'd never accepted it — because he used the present tense.

### Montage Editing

Use sharp scene jumps to create meaning. No smooth transitions — let the juxtaposition itself tell the story.

**How to execute:**
- Cut directly between two scenes; skip transitional phrases like "meanwhile" or "on the other hand"
- Mark the jump with a blank line or a divider
- Choose two images with strong contrast or resonance to each other
- The contrast produces a third meaning: A+B=C (C isn't on the page — it lives in the reader's head)

**Example:** A wedding toast cuts straight to a funeral. No explanation needed — the contrast itself tells the reader: someone has gone from joy to grief, time has passed, people have changed.

## Standard Chapter Structure

### 1. Opening Hook (first 20%) — the most important

**Must include:**
- Immediate conflict or crisis
- A significant event or piece of information
- Strong emotional impact
- Action

Use one of the ten opening techniques above, or combine them.

### 2. Development and Advance (middle 50–60%)

The chapter's core content — it must advance the plot or deepen the characters.

**How to advance:**
- **Reveal new information** — give the reader or the protagonist something important
- **Shift a relationship** — a relationship between characters changes
- **Escalate the problem** — the situation worsens, a new crisis appears
- **Develop the character** — the protagonist gains a new skill or new understanding

**Avoid:**
- Pure scene description (scenery, room layouts, and the like that serve no plot purpose)
- Character interactions with no bearing on the story
- Rehashing dialogue the reader already heard
- Replacing action with long stretches of interior monologue

### 3. Climax (last 15–20%)

The highest emotional or action point of the chapter.

**Types of climax:**
- **Action climax** — a fight, a chase, a confrontation
- **Emotional climax** — a major discovery, betrayal, confession, sacrifice
- **Psychological climax** — the protagonist's interior turning point

### 4. Closing Hook (last 5–10%)

Leave a source of suspense so the reader wants the next chapter. See [hook-techniques.md](hook-techniques.md).

## Chapter Type Categories

### Plot-Advancing Chapter

- **Purpose:** move the central story forward
- **Traits:** clear event progression, important information revealed
- **Examples:** the protagonist finds a clue, the enemy attacks, an ally betrays

### Character-Deepening Chapter

- **Purpose:** deepen the reader's understanding of a character
- **Traits:** reveal backstory, motive, internal conflict
- **Examples:** a flashback, an intimate conversation, a moment alone
- **Caution:** must stay connected to the main story — not a standalone character biography

### Atmosphere-Building Chapter

- **Purpose:** establish a specific mood or tension
- **Traits:** sensory description, careful pacing
- **Examples:** the calm before a storm, a stalking, waiting for a verdict

### Transition Chapter

- **Purpose:** link two major events
- **Traits:** information consolidation, shifts in location, time jumps
- **Caution:** keep it lean — avoid dragging; even a transition needs one small, irremovable event

## Controlling Chapter Pacing

These are directly actionable controls.

### Alternating Long and Short Sentences

Sentence length dictates reading speed. Short sentences are fast; long ones slow down.

**Action scenes — mostly short sentences:**
> He sidestepped. The blade kissed past the tip of his nose. Left foot plants, body rotates, point thrusts out. The other man never got to scream.

**Thought or description — mostly long sentences:**
> She sat by the window watching the people flow past below, and suddenly remembered how her mother used to stand on the balcony like this watching her off to school, and back then she'd taken her mother's gaze for granted, only now realizing how much it contained that she'd never learned to read.

**Operating rules:**
- Three consecutive sentences of the same length must be broken up
- Action passages: keep sentences under ten words
- Turning points: use one very short sentence to create a beat ("He froze." "No.")
- Consecutive short sentences are only allowed for lists and enumerations; everywhere else, vary the lengths

### Paragraph Breathing

Paragraphs are the reader's breathing rhythm. Short paragraphs, quick breaths; long paragraphs, slow ones.

- **Tense scenes:** one or two sentences a paragraph, or even one sentence per paragraph
- **Relaxed scenes:** five or six sentences a paragraph, room to expand
- **Sudden bursts:** in the middle of a long paragraph, drop in a single-sentence paragraph like a drumbeat

**Counter-example (uniform throughout):** Every paragraph about the same length reads like a bulldozer rolling over the reader — they won't notice why, but they'll be bored.

**Example (breathing varies):**
```
[Long] He spent the whole afternoon going through the files, every page read carefully, stopping now and then to jot a note. Outside, the light shifted from bright to amber, and he never thought to turn on a lamp. (relaxed, expansive)

[Short] The last page. A photograph fell out. (turning point, tightening)

[Short] It was her. (detonation, single line)
```

### Information-Density Waves

Information density = the amount of new information carried per unit of text.

```
High density → low density → high density → low density
(action/dialogue/reveal) → (settling/description/interior) → (new conflict/turning point) → (emotional digestion/scene transition)
```

**How to execute:**
- Every high-density passage must be followed by a low-density one so the reader can digest
- Three-plus high-density passages in a row = reader fatigue, skimming begins
- Three-plus low-density passages in a row = reader boredom, the book gets set down
- Each chapter should have 2–3 density waves — neither cranked to maximum throughout nor loose the whole way

### Reference Pacing Patterns

**Mystery/action rhythm:**
```
tension → brief release → more tension → climax → open-ended hook
```

**Romance rhythm:**
```
everyday interaction (light) → emotional shift (heating up) → misunderstanding or obstacle (tense) → truth or confession (climax) → a new hook
```

**Fantasy/Xianxia rhythm:**
```
cultivation/preparation (low density) → crisis arrives (medium density) → battle or breakthrough (high density) → post-battle digestion (low density) → new challenge emerges
```

### Full-Chapter Pacing Check

After writing a chapter, run this quick check:
1. Mark each paragraph's "speed" (fast / medium / slow)
2. Draw a pacing curve (visualize it)
3. If the curve is a flat line (uniform the whole way), it needs adjustment
4. If it's all fast with no slow passages, add breathing room
5. If it's all slow, find the conflict points you can accelerate

## Ensuring Continuity

Long-form novels have one most common failure mode: inconsistency. These mechanisms keep every chapter tightly meshed with the whole.

### Required Reading Before You Write

Before starting every new chapter:
1. Read the summaries of all completed chapters in `01-outline.md`
2. Read the last chapter file to know the current hook and emotional landing point
3. Check character state: location, emotion, relationships, known information

These three steps are not skippable. Skipping them is writing blind.

### Threading the Needle

Proactively build connections to the earlier narrative in each new chapter:
- Echo the foreshadowing and clues planted earlier
- Reference past events naturally (not like narration explaining — like the character's own memory)
- Keep character behavior consistent (consistent with personality, not repeating the same gestures)

### Tracking Character State

Track every important character's changes across chapters:
- **Location** — where they are, how they got there
- **Emotion** — what mood marked the end of the last chapter, does this chapter's opening pick it up
- **Relationships** — have their relationships with other characters changed
- **Capabilities** — have they gained new skills, information, or resources

Maintain a simple character-state table and update it each chapter.

### Managing New-Term Introductions

When a setting noun first appears (a skill, item, organization, term — e.g. "blue crystal"), the reader needn't know its full function, but they must learn its **category and one perceivable trait** on the spot. Suspense is the reader knowing what to ask; confusion is the reader not even knowing what the word is — the latter isn't leave-blank artistry, it's reading noise. Choose one of three options at first appearance:
- **Category + trait:** "a blue crystal the size of a thumbnail — on the black market more sought-after than gold"
- **Character reaction conveys importance:** bystanders draw in a sharp breath at the sight of it
- **Dialogue drops a hint of its nature:** "Keep that quiet — on the black market it could buy a man's life."

**Setting dictionary:** register every setting noun in `01-outline.md` — first-appearance chapter, what the reader knows, the full truth, the planned revealing chapter (format in [outline-template.md](outline-template.md)). Before writing each new chapter, cross-check: terms used this chapter — first appearances must come with a clue; established terms can't exceed what the "reader knows" column states; and anything reached its planned reveal chapter must deliver.

### Extending the Threads of Suspense

Make sure multiple threads of suspense advance in parallel:
- Each chapter answers at least one old question (even partially)
- Each chapter raises at least one new question or escalates an existing one
- Maintain a list of all unresolved threads; periodically check for forgotten leads

### Continuity Checklist

- [ ] Character behavior matches established personality; nothing jarring
- [ ] Earlier foreshadowing is paid off; the logic closes cleanly
- [ ] Highs and lows are distributed well; pacing is right
- [ ] Timeline is continuous; no time-jump errors
- [ ] Scene transitions are smooth; characters don't appear or vanish out of nowhere
- [ ] Character knowledge is consistent; they don't "forget" things they already know
- [ ] New terms first appear with contextual clues and are registered in the setting dictionary; established terms never exceed what the reader knows

## Chapter Length and Content Density

| Chapter type | Word range | Core events | Scenes | Best for |
|---------|---------|-------------|---------|---------|
| Short chapter | 1000-1500 | 1 major event | 1-2 scenes | Transitions, single-scene focus |
| Standard chapter | 1500-3000 | 2-3 major events | 2-4 scenes | Most chapters |
| Long chapter | 3000-4000 | 3-4 major events | 3-5 scenes | Climaxes, big battles, multi-thread resolution |

**Principles:**
- Each chapter contains at least one irremovable core event
- 1500–3000 words is the default target, allowed to flex with content
- Short chapters serve as an "abrupt stop" or "breathing space" — they shouldn't be the norm
- Long chapters are for major turning points, but must have enough substance behind them — no padding

## Genre-Specific Checks

Different genres carry their own hard requirements. Beyond the general checks, add the following per genre.

### Mystery/Thriller

- [ ] A clue is revealed (even a hidden one the reader only finds later)
- [ ] A new puzzle is raised (don't only answer, never ask)
- [ ] No logical holes (the reader must find no flaw on re-read)
- [ ] Suspects act on plausible motive (not made stupid to drive the plot)
- [ ] Fair-play: the reader can theoretically deduce the truth in advance

### Romance/Contemporary

- [ ] The relationship advances (no treading water for five chapters)
- [ ] There's romantic tension (ambiguity, misunderstanding, jealousy, butterflies — at least one)
- [ ] The reader cares whether these two end up together (if not, it's a failure)
- [ ] Sweet and angsty are balanced by story phase — not all sweet or all angsty
- [ ] Side characters have their own romantic philosophies; they don't exist to serve the leads

### Fantasy/Science Fiction

- [ ] The worldbuilding stays consistent (established rules aren't overturned)
- [ ] No setting contradictions (no convenient new rules invented on the spot)
- [ ] No over-explaining (let readers grasp rules through events, not blocks of exposition; but new terms still need their category clue on first appearance — see "Managing New-Term Introductions")
- [ ] The power system has clear boundaries (the protagonist can't grow infinitely strong)
- [ ] Novelty: at least one idea the reader finds genuinely interesting

### Action

- [ ] Action scenes are clear; the reader can visualize spatial positioning
- [ ] Fast pacing; action passages use short sentences and short paragraphs
- [ ] Geography is explicit (where they fight, which way they run, whether there's an escape route)
- [ ] Each fight has a different highlight — no identical combat every time
- [ ] Fights carry emotional stakes; not violence for its own sake

## Chapter Writing Checklist

Self-check every item after drafting each chapter. Fix anything that fails.

### Opening Check (most important)

- [ ] Is the first 20% extremely compelling? (If not, rewrite.)
- [ ] Is conflict or tension established by the first paragraph?
- [ ] Is there a significant event or information reveal?
- [ ] Strong emotional impact?
- [ ] Will readers urgently want to know what happens next?
- [ ] Have all "fatal mistakes" been avoided? (weather, daily routine, recap, etc.)

### Content Check

- [ ] Does this chapter advance the main plot or deepen a character?
- [ ] Is there conflict or a turning point?
- [ ] Is there a core event (deleting it would damage comprehension)?
- [ ] Does the dialogue advance plot or reveal character?
- [ ] Is key information shown rather than told?
- [ ] Does the ending leave a hook?
- [ ] Is ground prepared for the next chapter?

### Character Check

- [ ] Do characters act in keeping with established personality?
- [ ] Do characters react to events with emotion and action?
- [ ] Does dialogue distinguish different characters?
- [ ] Is personality expressed through action and dialogue, not stated directly?

### Dialogue Check

- [ ] Every line has a purpose (advancing plot, revealing character, creating conflict)
- [ ] No meaningless lines ("hi," "how are you," and the like)
- [ ] Dialogue is concise and natural, matching real speech
- [ ] There's subtext; not everything is said outright
- [ ] Dialogue tags used well; adverbs not overused

### Suspense Check

- [ ] The ending has a hook that makes the reader want the next chapter
- [ ] Suspense intensity matches the story's position
- [ ] No fake suspense (mechanical misunderstandings, meaningless "suddenly!")
- [ ] Conflict or anticipation is set up for the next chapter

### Show, Don't Tell Check

| Telling (avoid) | Showing (use) |
|-------------|-------------|
| He was furious | He gripped the table so hard his knuckles went white |
| She was beautiful | He stared at her, forget to speak |
| He was nervous | He kept adjusting his tie |
| The room was a mess | Clothes thrown on the sofa, takeout boxes stacked on the table |
| He was wealthy | He pulled a wad of cash from his pocket |
| She was heartbroken | She stirred her coffee, the spoon stopping for a long while |

- [ ] Are emotions stated outright? (Replace with physical reactions.)
- [ ] Are adjectives used to summarize? (Replace with specific description.)
- [ ] Are key scenes skipped? (Add the showing.)

### Pacing Check

- [ ] Sentence lengths vary; no three consecutive sentences of the same length
- [ ] Paragraph lengths vary; action scenes use short paragraphs
- [ ] Information density alternates between high and low
- [ ] The chapter's rhythm isn't a straight line (it has a wave feel)

### Continuity Check

- [ ] Continuous with earlier material; the last chapter's hook is answered
- [ ] Foreshadowing is paid off or about to be revealed
- [ ] Timeline consistent; event order correct
- [ ] Character knowledge is consistent

### Opening Self-Test

If the answer is "no" to any of the following, rewrite:
1. After the first three paragraphs, will the reader want to continue?
2. Does the opening contain conflict or crisis?
3. Does the opening contain surprise or a turning point?
4. Does the opening produce a strong emotion in the reader?
5. Does the opening avoid flat daily routine, weather, and background explanation?

## Language Quality Check

### Stripping Out "AI-Flavor"

AI-generated text carries recognizable language patterns that must be eliminated item by item:

**High-frequency AI vocabulary (fix each occurrence):**
- "Moreover," "however," "notably," "it must be emphasized," "not to be overlooked"
- "highlight," "embody," "empower," "mirror," "reflect"
- "can't help but," "welling up," "heart swelling"
- "at this moment," "as if," "like"

**Cliché stacking:**
- Using more than two stock parallel idioms in a row counts as stacking
- Method: search for all four-character/stock phrases in the text, break up runs or replace with plain informal speech

**Rigid sentence patterns:**
- "He did A. He did B. He did C." — three consecutive short sentences with the same subject
- Fix: merge, drop the subject, or vary the sentence structure

**Ban on empty "It was not X, but Y" constructions:**
- "The roar slammed into his eardrum — it wasn't thunder, it was deeper than thunder" → "The roar slammed into his eardrum, a sound deeper than thunder"
- "The sky split open. It wasn't the clouds tearing..." → "The sky split open..."

**Quantified hard limits (self-check on final draft; fix if exceeded):**
- "not A, but B" contrastive constructions: no more than 2 per chapter
- A single recurring image (snow, chess, walls — an image running through the work): no more than once per ~1,000 words
- The same signature gesture (fingering a clasp, rubbing a thumb, gripping a cup rim): no more than 8 times in the whole book
- Aphoristic endings (chapter-closing philosophical lines like "there was a crack, already open"): no more than 3 in the whole book, and no two consecutive chapters may end the same way
- Repeated care-giving beats (bringing food, handing over tea, tucking in the blanket): no more than 2 in the whole book — building warmth requires new actions or new meaning in old ones, not repeating the same gesture

### Managing "of" Density

- No more than two "of" constructions per sentence where avoidable
- Never the same possessive structure stacking in a string
- Simplify modifier chains wherever possible: "his red beautiful large coat" → "his red coat"

### Precise Word Choice

- Cut vague words: "some," "a certain," "probably," "seems" (unless deliberately signaling uncertainty)
- Replace the abstract with the concrete: "the weather was bad" → "muggy, the air clinging to his skin"
- Verbs outrank adjectives: "she walked fast" is weaker than "she was almost running"

## Quality Scoring

Score every chapter before delivery. Be objective; don't flatter yourself.

| Dimension | Score | Notes |
|-----|------|-----|
| Opening appeal | /10 | Do the first 3 paragraphs grab the reader? |
| Plot advance | /10 | Does the chapter advance the main line? |
| Character craft | /10 | Is character behavior consistent and deep? |
| Dialogue quality | /10 | Is the dialogue natural and plot-driving? |
| Suspense setup | /10 | Does the ending hook make the reader want more? |
| Pacing control | /10 | Tension and release in balance? |
| Show, don't tell | /10 | Action/dialogue rather than statement? |
| Language quality | /10 | No AI-flavor, precise word choice? |
| **Total** | **/80** | **>60 deliverable, >70 excellent** |

**How to use scoring:**
- Score each item against its checklist, not by feel
- If any item scores below 6, fix that dimension and re-score
- Total below 60: the whole chapter needs major revision
- Total 60–70: basically deliverable; polish the details
- Total above 70: quality passes; can be delivered
